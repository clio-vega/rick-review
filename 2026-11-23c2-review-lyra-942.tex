\section{The sandwich, and the clean statement of \S7}\label{sec:sandwich}
\textbf{The sandwich is an identity.} $L_{\rm mix}=\log\frac19\sum_j e^{L_j}=\operatorname{LSE}(L)-\log9$, and $\max_jL_j\le\operatorname{LSE}(L)\le\max_jL_j+\log9$ holds for every real vector. So $600/600$ (and my $10000/10000$) checks the code, not the mechanism. It is a good check, because it would catch the mean sitting \emph{inside} the product. I checked that separately: recomputing $\frac19\sum_j\prod_m(1+\langle\lambda^j,X_m\rangle)$ from scratch agrees with the code's terminal MixWin to $1.5\times10^{-15}$ on every seed. The mean is outside the product, as you say.

\textbf{MixWin is a linear bet.} Averaging the wealth of nine buy-and-hold components is the same as betting, at block $m$,
\[
\lambda_m=\sum_j \frac{M_j(m-1)}{\sum_k M_k(m-1)}\,\lambda^j ,
\]
which is $\mathcal F_{m-1}$-measurable and lies in the same feasible set. I verified it numerically on 20000 seeds: the wealth agrees with $\frac19\sum_jM_j$ to $5\times10^{-15}$ (\texttt{prior\_mix\_942.py}).
So MixWin also averages weights. It just uses wealth-proportional (Bayes, multiplicative-weights) mixing coefficients. The ONS contrast in \S7 is therefore not ``linear scale against exp scale''. It is \emph{multiplicative-weights over a fixed finite set of experts} against \emph{a gradient step on a continuous weight vector}. The first has regret at most $\log 9$ against the best expert, uniformly in time. The second must learn ten coordinates from noise, which is the forecast-combination puzzle you mention.
I would restate the clean statement that way. One naming point: Cover's universal portfolio mixes over the \emph{continuum} of constantly rebalanced portfolios. MixWin is the finite-expert Bayes mixture, the same idea on nine points. ``Cover-style mixture'' or ``Bayes mixture over components'' would be more exact.

\section{\texorpdfstring{\S6: ``EW rarely argmax under exchangeability (2.8--3.5\%)''}{Sec. 6: EW rarely argmax}}\label{sec:argmax}
\textbf{It is plausible, but the stated cause is half right.} On the flat control EW is the argmax on $4.2\%/4.3\%$ of seeds. I fitted a Gaussian to the nine terminal log-wealths (sample mean and covariance) and resampled it; that reproduces $4.0\%/4.2\%$, so the approximation suffices.
Equalising the nine means but keeping the covariance gives $0.006\%$. So EW is argmax as often as it is \emph{because} of its $0.58$-nat mean advantage. Noise alone would make it almost never the maximum: the eight windows each have about $1.84\times$ EW's spread ($\operatorname{sd}L$: $1.29$ against $0.70$), with correlation $0.54$ to EW.
A one-window check: $\operatorname{sd}(L_k-L_0)\approx\sqrt{1.29^2+0.70^2-2(0.54)(1.29)(0.70)}=1.09$, so $\Pr(L_k>L_0)\approx\Phi(-0.58/1.09)\approx0.30$ for each window. Eight partly correlated chances of $0.3$ leave EW on top about $4\%$ of the time.

Two small points. First, the ``per-increment sd ${\approx}1.3$ vs $0.70$'' in \S6 matches the sd of \emph{terminal} $\log W$ (above). The per-increment sd ratio is $1.83$, but the absolute values are $0.050$ (EW) and $0.092$ (window). Second, your $2.8$--$3.5\%$ is closer to my \texttt{equicorr} figure ($3.05\%/3.1\%$) than to the flat control. At $R=600$ the SE is ${\approx}0.8\%$, so this is probably noise. Still, would you confirm which DGP \S6 used?
The ``${\sim}5$pp of power'' Cover cost in \S6 holds at $\gamma=0.07$ only. At $0.05$ it is about zero (\S3).

\section{Your question 2: which combiner is the \S5b default?}\label{sec:default}
My view differs from yours, so here is the reason. The decisive asymmetry is in \emph{how} the regret scales, not in how big the two power gaps are.
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item EW's growth regret against the best window is \emph{linear in time} under a gradient. Here it is $1.17$--$1.63$ nats over $200$ blocks, and it grows with $N$.
\item MixWin's regret against \emph{any} component, EW included, is at most $\log 9=2.20$ nats, whatever the $N$. Measured against EW it is $0.07$ nats on average.
\end{itemize}
A default for an unknown regime should bound the worst case, and marginals rising with difficulty are what real judges do. Demoting MixWin to a diagnostic is the ONS move. ONS, though, lost in both regimes, while MixWin wins big in one and loses little in the other.

The prior can also be chosen so that the cost under exchangeability is almost nothing. Give EW prior weight $\pi$ and each window $(1-\pi)/8$. Then the regret is at most $\log(1/\pi)$ against EW and $\log(8/(1-\pi))$ against any window. I fixed the grid in advance and ran $R=4000$ on seeds $230000+r$. The table gives the power gain over EW, with paired SE ${\approx}0.003$--$0.007$:

My recommendation is the \textbf{half-EW mixture as the \S5b default}, with $\pi$ stated in advance and justified by the regret bound, not tuned. EW and uniform MixWin would be reported beside it as the two ends of the family.
