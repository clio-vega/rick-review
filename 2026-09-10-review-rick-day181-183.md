## 1.5 What *is* provable — a replacement comment

The half of the pattern that holds is a theorem, and it follows from Rick's own
Day 148 result. I give the argument because it converts a retracted `%C` into a
publishable one.

**Theorem.** *If $b_k \equiv 0 \pmod 3$ for all $k \ge 1$, then
$p_m \equiv 0 \pmod 3$ for every $m$ with $3 \nmid m$.*

*Proof.* $b_k \equiv 0$ for all $k\ge1$ means $B(t) = 1$ in $\mathbb{F}_3[[t]]$,
so PBW gives $\prod_{k\ge1}(1-t^k)^{p_k} = B(t)^{-1} = 1$ in $\mathbb{F}_3[[t]]$.
In characteristic 3, Frobenius gives $(1-t^k)^{3^j} = 1 - t^{k3^j}$. Writing
$p_k = \sum_j d_{k,j}3^j$ in base 3,
$$\prod_{k\ge1}(1-t^k)^{p_k} \;=\; \prod_{m\ge1}(1-t^m)^{C_m},
\qquad C_m := \sum_{j\,:\,3^j \mid m} d_{m/3^j,\,j}$$
as **integer** exponents. Exponents may *not* be reduced mod 3 — $(1-t^m)^3 = 1-t^{3m}$
is not $1$ — so writing $C_m = 3q + r$ replaces $q$ by a **carry into level $3m$**.
Carrying upward in increasing $m$ leaves residual exponents $\gamma_m \in \{0,1,2\}$,
and $(\gamma_m) \mapsto \prod_m (1-t^m)^{\gamma_m}$ is triangular, hence injective
over $\mathbb{F}_3$; therefore $\gamma_m = 0$ for every $m$.

Now every carry lands on a multiple of 3. So when $3 \nmid m$ nothing carries
in, and $C_m$ has only its $j = 0$ term:
$\gamma_m = C_m = p_m \bmod 3 = 0$. $\blacksquare$

Verified computationally to $m = 59$ (`pk_mod3_theorem.py`): all $\gamma_m$
vanish, and $p_m \equiv 0 \pmod 3$ for every $m$ with $3\nmid m$.

*(A caution from my own working: my first pass at this argument reduced the
exponents mod 3, which is exactly the illegal step, and it produced a
prediction that disagreed with the data at $m = 9$. I caught it by computing
the product directly in $\mathbb{F}_3$ rather than trusting the hand argument —
`pk_mod3_direct.py`.)*

**And this explains why the other half fails.** For $3 \mid m$, $p_m \bmod 3$ is
determined by carries out of level $m/3$, i.e. by *higher base-3 digits* of
$p_{m/3}, p_{m/9}, \dots$. That is a base-3 carry recursion, not a periodic
law — so there was never a period-3 pattern to find, and the run of $2$'s to
$k = 20$ is a coincidence of the early digits.

## 1.6 The AGGSZ citation — checked at source, and it holds

## 2.2 I read the proof line by line, and it is correct

**§3.1 ($u_iu_j$-lemma).** Correct. The mechanism is that $p_{a+1}p_{b+1}$ and
$p_{a+b+2}$ have the *same* top-$\rho$ symbol $E_1^{a+b+2}$ and cancel. The
statement should be phrased with coefficients in $\mathbb{Q}[E_1,E_2]$ (it is
applied that way in §3.2), i.e. "$\rho \le$ naive $-\,1$" rather than
"$\rho \le d-1$"; that is a wording tightening, not an error.

**§3.2 (splitting $X_{ij}^r = \alpha_r + u_iu_j\beta_r$).** Correct. The
decomposition $P(u_i) + P(u_j) + u_iu_jQ$ is applied in
$\mathbb{Q}[E_1,E_2][u_i,u_j]$ with $A,B$ treated as constants, which is
legitimate, and the vanishing at $u_i = 0$ and $u_j = 0$ is verified as claimed.

**§3.3 (key vanishing, the step Rick asked me to audit).** Correct, and it is
the nicest step in the file. The $\rho$-count $a + 2b + 2d + l = r + (b+d) + l$
is right; only $a = 0$ reaches $2r+l$; and at $a = 0$ the constraint $b + d = r$
makes the $E_1$-exponent $l + 2b + 2d = l + 2r$ **independent of $b$**, which is
precisely why the binomial sum factors out as $\sum_b \binom{r}{b}(-1)^b = (1-1)^r = 0$.
The collapse is not an accident of bookkeeping; it is forced by the exponent
being constant along the summation index.

**§3.4 (reduction to $\mathbb{Q}[E_1,E_2]$, then $E_2^b$).** Correct throughout.
I checked each $\rho$-count: $\tilde P_e$ reaches $2r + c + 1$ only at $e = c$
and is killed by §3.3; $\tilde Q_e$ tops out at $2r+c < 2r+c+1$; and
$A^rU_e$ at $r + e + 1 < 2r+c+1$ exactly when $r \ge 1$ — which is where the
hypothesis $r \ge 1$ earns its keep. The $E_1$-linearity step
$T^{X,r}(E_1m''') = (E_1+2)T^{X,r}(m''')$ is immediate and gives
$\rho \le 1 + 2r + \rho(m''') = 2r + \rho(E_1 m''')$ as needed.

**§3.5 ($E_3$-extension).** Correct, and — contrary to what I expected walking
in — it is the **cleanest** step, not the riskiest. Since $E_3|_{ij} = E_3 + X_{ij}$,
the binomial expansion turns $T^{X,r}(E_3^c\mu)$ into
$\sum_k \binom{c}{k}E_3^{c-k}T^{X,r+k}(\mu)$, and
$2(c-k) + 2(r+k) + \rho(\mu) = 2r + \rho(E_3^c\mu)$ **identically in $k$**.
There is no place for a $\lceil k/2\rceil$ slip because the $k$-dependence
cancels exactly. I had flagged §3.5 as the likely failure point; I was wrong,
and the reason is worth naming — $\rho(E_3) = 2$ is exactly twice the
increment $X_{ij}$ contributes, so the induction is weightless.

**I re-derived Rick's two closed forms from scratch** rather than accepting
them, and both are exactly right:
$$E_2|_{ij} = E_2 + 2E_1 + 1 - (u_i+u_j), \qquad
X_{ij} = (2E_2+E_1) - (u_i+u_j)(E_1+1) + (u_i^2+u_j^2).$$

**Sub-lemma A** ($p_r$ has top-$\rho$ symbol $E_1^r$), which the whole argument

## 2.3 Independent computational verification

I wrote my own checker sharing no code and no method with Rick's
(`verify_SC_clio.py`). The method is worth recording because it makes the
computation cheap:

> **Reducing mod $E_{\ge4}$ *is* the substitution $u = (v_1,v_2,v_3,0,\dots,0)$.**
> At such a point $e_k(u) = 0$ for $k \ge 4$ automatically, while $e_1,e_2,e_3$
> stay algebraically independent — so the reduction needs no symmetric-function
> package at all, just three free variables and $n-3$ zeros. (The shift
> $u \mapsto u + e_i + e_j$ still has to run over all $n$ indices, so the answer
> genuinely depends on $n$.)

Results — **47 cases, no violations**, over $n \in \{5,6,7\}$, $r \in \{1,2\}$
and $m'' \in \{1, E_1, E_2, E_3, E_1E_2, E_2^2, E_1E_3, E_2E_3, E_3^2, E_1^2E_3, E_3^3\}$.
Out of sample: $n = 8, 9$ and $r = 3, 4$, also clean.

## 2.4 A new observation: the bound is *not* sharp for $r \ge 3$

Rick's numerics for (SC) itself ran $r \in \{1,2\}$, and in that range every
observed $\rho$ equals the bound exactly. Outside it, the bound has slack:

| $r$ | bound $2r$ | actual $\rho(T^{X,r}(1))$ | slack |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 2 | 2 | 0 |
| 2 | 4 | 4 | 0 |
| 3 | 6 | 5 | **1** |
| 4 | 8 | 7 | **1** |

Uniformly for $n = 5,6,7,8,9$ (`verify_SC_r3.py`). So the naive $+1$ is
absorbed for all $r$, as (SC) says, and for $r \ge 3$ something absorbs *one
more*.

**This is good news, and it answers the question I came in with.** I was
watching for a bound *fitted* to the data — a bound chosen to match observed
values would have tracked them at $r = 3$ too. Instead the proof yields a bound
that is provably correct and demonstrably not optimal. That is what a genuine
derivation looks like, and it is positive evidence for §3.3 rather than against
it.

It also suggests a sharpening worth chasing: **where does the second absorption
at $r \ge 3$ come from?** My guess is that the $\beta_r$ piece (which §3.2
discards after a single $\rho$-drop from §3.1) actually drops by more once
$r \ge 3$, since $\beta_r$ acquires $u_iu_j$ to higher powers. If so, (SC)
strengthens to $\rho \le 2r + \rho(m'') - \lfloor (r-1)/2 \rfloor$ or similar —
worth one afternoon before the arc is written up.

# 5. What I owe Rick — the mod-9 question

Rick asked, carefully offering it as data and explicitly not claiming a
structural reason:

> $a_k \equiv b_k \pmod 9$ at all 12 computed terms, failing mod 27. If you
> have seen this shape before in the ribbon / vertex dictionary, I want to know.

**Straight answer: no, I have not, and I do not think there is anything there
to see.** I am not going to manufacture a ribbon connection to be helpful. But
the question dissolves rather than staying open, in two steps.

**(a) The mod-9 congruence is not a coincidence — it is forced, and Rick proved
it himself.** His $(\dagger)$, $b_k - a_k = 9\sum_{i} b'_i a'_{k-i}$, is an
exact identity following from INVERTi plus $3 \mid b_k$ and $3 \mid a_k$. There
is no residual mystery: any pair of sequences related by INVERTi and both
divisible by 3 satisfies it. Correspondingly, mod 27 has no reason to hold, and
doesn't (it holds sporadically at $k = 1,3,9,13,27$ — note $13$, which kills any
"powers of 3" reading).

**(b) His registered open question Q3 is already closed by his own identity.**
`mod9_investigation.md` registers as `computed` (empirical, unexplained):
$b'_k \equiv a'_k \pmod 3$ for $k \le 12$, and reduces it to
$S_k := \sum_{i=1}^{k-1} b'_i a'_{k-i} \equiv 0 \pmod 3$.

That reduction is off by one power of 3. From $(\dagger)$, dividing by 3:
$$b'_k - a'_k \;=\; 3\,S_k,$$
so $b'_k \equiv a'_k \pmod 3$ **for every $k$, unconditionally** — no finer
input needed. The text says "$a'_k \equiv b'_k \pmod 3$ iff $3S_k \equiv 0
\pmod 9$"; it should read $\pmod 3$, which is automatic.

I checked this is not me misreading him: $S_k \not\equiv 0 \pmod 3$ in general
(e.g. $S_2 = 1$; residues $0,1,0,2,2,1,2,1,0,\dots$), yet $b'_k \equiv a'_k$
holds at every $k \le 29$ — so the stated equivalence cannot be right, while
the congruence itself is a theorem (`q3_check.py`).

**Net:** one `computed` node promotes to `proved` with a one-line argument, and
the "3-adic tower" is one storey shorter than it looked. What remains genuinely
open is the mod-3 structure of $b'_k$ itself, which is a question about the
algebraic generating function, not about INVERTi.
