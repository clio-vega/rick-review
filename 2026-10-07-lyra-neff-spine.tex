\section{Half two: the statistic cannot answer the question}

Three results, in increasing order of how much they close off.

\subsection{It does not collapse toward zero --- it sits on its own floor}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:floor}
Let $R$ be any $I\times J$ real matrix each of whose rows sums to zero (i.e.\ any
per-item-demeaned residual matrix), let $G=R^{\top}R$, $d_j=\sqrt{G_{jj}}>0$. Then
\[
  \frac{1}{J(J-1)}\sum_{j\ne j'}\frac{G_{jj'}}{d_jd_{j'}}\;\ge\;-\frac{1}{J-1},
\]
with equality iff all $d_j$ are equal. Moreover, with the \emph{pooled}
normalisation $\bar g=\frac1J\sum_jG_{jj}$ in place of $d_jd_{j'}$, the left side
equals $-\frac{1}{J-1}$ \emph{identically} --- for every such $R$, with no sampling
error at all.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Row sums zero means $R\mathbf 1=0$, so $\mathbf 1^{\top}G\mathbf 1=\|R\mathbf
1\|^2=0$, i.e.\ $\sum_{j\ne j'}G_{jj'}=-\sum_jG_{jj}=-J\bar g$. Dividing by
$J(J-1)\bar g$ gives the pooled identity. For the Pearson form put $u_j=1/d_j$; then
$\sum_{j\ne j'}G_{jj'}u_ju_{j'}=u^{\top}Gu-\sum_jG_{jj}u_j^2=u^{\top}Gu-J\ge-J$
since $G\succeq0$. Equality needs $u\in\ker G$, and $\ker G\ni\mathbf 1$ with
$u\propto\mathbf 1$ iff all $d_j$ coincide.
\end{proof}

\noindent Numerically: the pooled form reproduces $-1/(J-1)$ to machine precision on
all three subsets, and over $4000$ random trials (Gaussian and binary,
heteroscedastic, $J$ from $3$ to $19$) the floor is never violated --- the minimum of
$(\text{statistic}+1/(J-1))$ is exactly $0$.

So the number to compare against is $-1/31=-0.0323$, not $0$. Measured: alphaNLI
$-0.0310$, SNLI $-0.0293$, MNLI-m $-0.0302$. \textbf{Reading $-0.031$ as
``$\approx0$, therefore independent'' mistakes the floor for the origin.} The
distance from the floor is the AM--GM gap in the per-judge residual variances, and
nothing else.

\subsection{The shared-prior parameter is exactly invisible}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:dead}
Suppose, conditional on item $i$, the error vector has exchangeable covariance
$\Sigma_i=s_i^2\bigl[(1-\rho)\mathrm{Id}_J+\rho\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^{\top}\bigr]$ for a
common $\rho$. Let $\Cc=\mathrm{Id}_J-\tfrac1J\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^{\top}$ be the
per-item demeaning projector. Then
\[
  \Cc\,\Sigma_i\,\Cc \;=\; s_i^2\,(1-\rho)\,\Cc .
\]
Hence $\rho$ enters the residual covariance \emph{only as a scalar factor}, the
residual \emph{correlation} matrix is $\tfrac{J}{J-1}\Cc$ regardless of $\rho$, and
every off-diagonal entry is $-1/(J-1)$ for every admissible $\rho$. In the Gaussian
case $R^{(\rho)}\overset{d}{=}\sqrt{1-\rho}\,R^{(0)}$, so the sampling distribution
of \emph{any} scale-invariant functional of the residual correlation matrix is
\emph{identical} under $\rho=0$ and under any $\rho$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
$\Cc\mathbf 1=0$ and $\Cc^2=\Cc$, so $\Cc\Sigma_i\Cc=s_i^2(1-\rho)\Cc\,\mathrm{Id}\,\Cc
+s_i^2\rho\,(\Cc\mathbf 1)(\Cc\mathbf 1)^{\top}=s_i^2(1-\rho)\Cc$. Mixing over
items replaces $s_i^2$ by $\mathbb E[s_i^2]$ and preserves proportionality to $\Cc$.
\end{proof}

\noindent Verified symbolically at $J=3,4,5,9$ ($\Cc\Sigma\Cc-s^2(1-\rho)\Cc$ is the
zero matrix), with $\partial_\rho(\text{residual correlation})=0$, and numerically
$-0.25$ at $J=5$ for $\rho\in\{0,0.1,0.3,0.5,0.9,0.99\}$.

\begin{corollary}
No statistic computed from the per-item-demeaned residual correlation matrix has any
power against exchangeable conditional dependence --- at any $I$, any $J$. The power
curve is flat at the size. The requested minimum detectable
$\rho_{\mathrm{shared}}$ does not exist, and a larger panel does not help.
\end{corollary}

\noindent The reason is geometric and I think it is the sentence worth keeping: a
shared prior is a rank-one perturbation \emph{in the $\mathbf 1$ direction}, and
per-item demeaning is precisely the projector that annihilates $\mathbf 1$. The
statistic was asked to detect the one thing the transformation in front of it deletes.
This is the transpose of a defect already in my document: $\omega^2$ is annihilated by
the per-\emph{column} centring inside Pearson correlation (Thm.~4), so $\hat\varphi$
reads $0.5001$ across $\omega^2\in\{0,\dots,25\}$. Same projector, other index. Note
also that Prop.~\ref{prop:dead} holds for $\rho<0$ too, down to the admissible
$-1/(J-1)$: even conditional \emph{anti}-coordination is invisible.

\subsection{Deeper: for exchangeable judges the two hypotheses are one model}

If judges are exchangeable within an item, each row is an exchangeable binary
sequence, and a positively correlated exchangeable sequence is (de~Finetti) a
mixture of i.i.d.\ Bernoulli --- i.e.\ conditionally \emph{independent} given a
row-level latent. ``Hard item'' and ``shared prior'' are then the same statistical
model with the latent relabelled; the distinction is causal and semantic, not
distributional, and \emph{no} statistic on an $I\times J$ matrix recovers it.

\begin{remark}
The honest limit of this argument: de~Finetti's representation needs infinite
exchangeability, and finite exchangeable sequences need not be mixtures of i.i.d.
So strictly, this closes off the extendible part of the alternative. The sliver it
leaves is non-extendible (negatively correlated) exchangeable structure --- which
Prop.~\ref{prop:dead} shows is invisible to the residual correlation matrix anyway.
Between the two results the exchangeable family is fully accounted for, by
Prop.~\ref{prop:dead} exactly and by de~Finetti up to that caveat.
\end{remark}

\subsection{And the little information it does carry runs backwards}

The statistic is not literally constant: the AM--GM gap responds to judge
heteroscedasticity, which real dependence induces. Against an \emph{exact}
within-row permutation null (\S\ref{sec:repose}), alphaNLI reads $-0.030953$ versus a
null mean of $-0.031896$ (sd $5.0\times10^{-5}$), $z=+19$, $p\le0.002$; SNLI
$z=+129$; MNLI-m $z=+324$. The departures are real and utterly negligible in
magnitude --- the entire dynamic range here is $\sim10^{-3}$ --- but note the
\emph{sign}: the observed value is \emph{closer to zero} than the null. More
structure pushes the statistic toward zero. \textbf{So even the residual
information in this statistic runs opposite to the intended inference: ``nearer
zero'' is evidence \emph{against} conditional independence, not for it.}

\section{The question reposed, and answered}\label{sec:repose}

\subsection{The right null and the right statistic}

\textbf{Null.} Your hypothesis is ``judge labels carry no information beyond the
item.'' If so, every within-row permutation of the error matrix is equally likely,
which gives an \emph{exact} finite-sample null with no model and no fitted difficulty
distribution. Two versions:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{N1}, fixed row sums: tests judge main effect $+$ judge clustering.
\item \textbf{N2}, fixed row \emph{and} column sums (Strona et al.\ curveball): tests
clustering \emph{beyond} the main effect. Since row and column sums are the
sufficient statistics of the additive item-plus-judge (Rasch) model, N2 is its exact
conditional test. This is the decisive one.
\end{itemize}
This null contains both conditional independence \emph{and} exchangeable conditional
dependence of any strength --- so the test is blind to exactly what
\S4.3 shows is unidentifiable, and sensitive to exactly what is not. That is the
coherence I wanted before trusting it.

\textbf{Statistic.} ESDOF (participation ratio) of the residual correlation matrix.
Its population null value is \emph{exactly} $J-1$: under the null the residual
correlation matrix is $\tfrac{J}{J-1}\Cc$, whose spectrum is $\tfrac{J}{J-1}$ with
multiplicity $J-1$ and a single $0$, so
$(\sum\lambda)^2/\sum\lambda^2=J^2/(J^2/(J-1))=J-1$. It measures \emph{flatness} of
the residual spectrum, which is what ``no structure beyond the item'' actually means.
The finite-sample null at $I\approx1000,J=32$ is $27.6$, not $31$ --- which is why the
permutation null is necessary and the algebra alone would have overstated the effect.

\subsection{What the structure is}

\textbf{Vendor families are real.} Grouping the 32 judges into 10 vendors, the mean
\emph{residual} correlation within vendor is $+0.0761$ against $-0.0409$ between
vendor, a gap of $+0.1170$; permuting the vendor labels among judges gives
$p=5\times10^{-5}$. SNLI: $+0.0985/-0.0412$, gap $+0.1397$, $p=5\times10^{-5}$.
MNLI-m: $+0.0653/-0.0391$, gap $+0.1044$, $p=9\times10^{-4}$.

\textbf{But vendor is not the whole deficit.} A pure vendor-block alternative tuned
to reproduce the observed gap $+0.117$ predicts ESDOF $\approx26.9$; the data shows
$21.24$. So most of the deficit is \emph{not} vendor-aligned. The leading residual
eigenvalue is $4.11$ against a flat null of $1.032$, and its loading correlates
$+0.78$ with the judge's own error rate (SNLI $+0.64$, MNLI-m $+0.57$).
\textbf{That looks like a capability axis: weaker judges fail together on the same
items, beyond what item difficulty predicts.} I flag this as the interpretation I am
least confident in --- vendor explains $R^2=0.57$ of that same loading, so vendor and
capability are badly confounded here and I have not separated them.

\textbf{Minimum detectable effect, for the alternative that is detectable.} Against a
vendor-block alternative, the ESDOF omnibus reaches $80\%$ power at
$\rho_{\mathrm{blk}}\approx0.25$, i.e.\ an observable within-vendor excess residual
correlation of $\approx0.134$. The targeted vendor-label test is far more sensitive
($p=5\times10^{-5}$ at an excess of $0.117$) --- as a targeted test should be. Use
ESDOF as the omnibus screen and the label-permutation test when you have a candidate
grouping.

\section{What I am least sure about}
